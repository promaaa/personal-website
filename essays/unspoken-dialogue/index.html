The Unspoken Dialogue

On the grounding honesty of physical effort and its dialogue with the intellectual mind.

The Unspoken Dialogue

We live in an age that celebrates the disembodied mind. We read, design, and calculate as though the body could simply carry our brain from one chair to another. Yet this brain accounts for only about 2% of our body weight and around 20% of our energy use at rest. So what has become of the rest?

Some of my best ideas come while walking or going for an easy run. But what interests me more is the dialogue that physical effort forces me to have with myself.

For me, it first took the form of sprinting. Years spent repeating movements for a few seconds of running. I had to learn to feel poor posture, a foot staying on the ground too long, unnecessary tension. Understanding the movement was not enough: I had to be able to perform it. And during those few seconds, it was hard to think about anything else.

Then the dialogue changed. I moved on to endurance running. The effort lasted long enough for me to observe my reactions: the urge to start too fast, impatience, fatigue, and the negotiation that begins when I would rather be somewhere else. I had to learn to distinguish between what called for slowing down and what simply called for a little patience.

Training for a race brings this conversation into my daily life. Nobody checks whether I went for my long run that winter Sunday. I find myself alone with my target paces and my shoes, still damp from the day before. As the weeks pass, I learn more about what I can ask of myself and what I can sustain.

I spend a lot of time working on abstract problems. Sport reminds me that I can have a very convincing explanation and still be wrong about my own abilities. It makes me listen, try, and adjust. My body, too, has a part in what I learn.

And sport is also about feeling alive, being out of breath, feeling my legs, and coming home exhausted and happy. After a day spent using my brain, I enjoy reconnecting with the rest.

Marc Duboc
